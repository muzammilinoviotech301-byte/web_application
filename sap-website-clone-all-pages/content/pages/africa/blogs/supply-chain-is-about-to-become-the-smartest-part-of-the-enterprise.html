The supply chain is about to become the smartest part of the enterprise

As AI agents begin orchestrating sourcing, production, and logistics, companies could gain a competitive edge by ethically responding to disruptions in minutes rather than days.

According to the KPMG 2026 Transforming the Enterprise: Supply Chain report, AI adoption is expanding quickly across supply chain functions. Among these leaders, 44% report that AI is already embedded in logistics, 43% in inventory management and 41% in demand planning. This article explores how to think about the evolving role of AI and the impact on supply chain practitioners and executives.

“In five years, the supply chain will not be a back-office function anymore,” predicts Hernan De la Torre, KPMG’s US SAP Supply Chain Offering Leader. “It will be the most intelligent system in the enterprise; companies that learn how to orchestrate first will define the standard, and everybody else will have to follow.”

The next generation of industry supply chain leaders will not be defined by how fast they decide, but by how well they decide at speed as accuracy, explainability, sovereignty, and trust become essential markers for their people and their customers.  As De la Torre explained, “Speed without ethics is not leadership, and the real advantage will belong to those who build ethical resilience” which is the ability to move fast and stay accountable for the consequences of supplying chain jobs, in the real physical world, and society.

But this transformation raises a challenge that extends beyond technology. As AI amplifies human capacity and skill, work that was once bound by what a single person could handle opens up. Employers will need to think ahead about how they identify, cultivate and prepare the next generation of professionals in this space.

What would it take to make that happen? On a recent episode of The Future of Supply Chain podcast, De la Torre, Principal, and Stefan Seitz, a Director at KPMG Germany, discuss all this, and how AI agents, trusted data, and human expertise could reshape supply chain management.

When decisions take minutes instead of days

Imagine two manufacturers facing the same port disruption. One spends endless hours evaluating alternative routes and revising production schedules. The second uses AI agents to prepare a coordinated response within minutes, giving planners the context, options, and consequences in one view, and positions the organization several steps ahead of everybody else.

This scenario illustrates the potential of AI-driven supply chain orchestration, where AI agents synchronize decisions across traditionally independent and isolating functions.

“Integration connects systems, modules, and functions together. Orchestration is about synchronizing decisions,” advises De la Torre. As he explained “That distinction is the core of KPMG’s Neural Supply Chain Orchestration (NSCO™), the framework behind how we help organizations move from integrated systems to synchronized decisions”.

Take, for example, rising oil prices, new tariffs, and geopolitical uncertainty. All these economic factors can force companies to reconsider recent decisions involving transportation, sourcing, and production. AI agents could evaluate these interconnected challenges, simulate alternatives, and recommend coordinated responses.

“At the moment, our customers are also asking us questions such as, ‘Do you think the next ship will still go through the Suez Canal? Is it going to pass? Is it not?’” Seitz explained.

Answering questions like these requires evaluating enormous amounts of information, often under intense time pressure. AI can help businesses weigh their options and respond before conditions change again, potentially protecting costs, customer commitments, and operational continuity.

How AI Will Reshape Supply Chain Management, Jobs, and Competitive Advantage

The shift toward intelligent orchestration will also change what it means to work in supply chain management.

Seitz sees a future where planners oversee networks of AI agents that gather information, run simulations, and prepare decisions. Their job moves toward evaluating trade-offs, guiding operations, and deciding which actions should move forward. “Helping to steer the army of agents, collecting information for us, helping prepare the decisions,” as he puts it. AI presents the options and their consequences, while people retain responsibility for making the final call.

But that arrangement raises a difficult challenge for long-term workforce development. Many experienced supply chain professionals built their expertise through years of managing transactions, resolving operational problems, and learning how decisions affect the business. Those early responsibilities prepared them for more advanced roles.

With AI assuming more of that work, how will junior employees get the experience they need to become leaders in the field?

De la Torre believes keeping humans involved in certain processes may be necessary, even when AI could complete the tasks independently. Human oversight sometimes needs to be deliberately preserved “for the benefit of the education of those future planners or buyers,” he remarks. The next generation of supply chain executives will not just operate the orchestration layer. They will have had to grow inside it.

That is a design decision, not an accident, and the companies that make it on purpose will build the deeper bench.

Could AI make Supply Chain solutions more valuable than ever?

Supply chain professionals will increasingly rely on AI-driven recommendations, making the accuracy of the underlying information a necessity. Even the most sophisticated AI agents can make costly mistakes when working with inaccurate or incomplete data.

That raises another question: What happens to the enterprise systems responsible for managing that information when AI becomes more deeply embedded in supply chain operations?

De la Torre believes ERP and supply chain systems will play an even more critical role. “We're far from the end of the ERP era, as some analysts may like to say,” he assures. To support decisions affecting factories, warehouses, and transportation networks, AI agents will need accurate operational information, consistent governance, and reliable systems of record. The ERP is not disappearing; it is being asked to carry more weight.

Seitz takes that argument further by suggesting that AI could unlock the full potential of business systems. He envisions “ERP coming to its full fruition, to its full capability, with the use of AI.”

The combination also opens a new category of role: the business orchestrator, the layer between the digital core and the physical world where humans and AI co-decide. In practice, that means device-agnostic agents operating inside workflows that stay sovereign, auditable, and accountable, from the ERP all the way out to the field. It is where ambitious supply chain processes become possible, not just demos, especially in the places where connectivity and native enterprise devices do not yet exist.

The benefits extend across the chain, from accelerating decisions and lowering costs to building resilience. With reliable information flowing across the network, companies can reduce buffer inventory, limit expedited freight, and make better trade-offs between sourcing, transportation, and production. The enterprise systems supporting those decisions become even more critical to business performance when AI assumes greater responsibility.

Early movers could set the competitive standard

Intelligent supply chains will change how companies compete, giving organizations that adapt quickly an advantage in navigating uncertainty, managing costs, and responding to emerging opportunities.

Getting there will require businesses to rethink how technology, data, and people work together. Decisions about AI autonomy, human oversight, and workforce development will separate the companies that pull ahead from the companies that fall behind.

If De la Torre's and Seitz's predictions prove correct, the next generation of industry leaders may be defined as much by the intelligence of their supply chains as by the products they make or sell. And the legacy of the executives who build those supply chains will be measured by two things at once: the speed at which they moved, and the trust they built along the way.

Speed without trust is a short run. Speed with trust is a standard.
