AI is taking over supply chain decisions and why that could be good news for humans

As AI takes on more supply chain decisions, employees could gain something valuable: more time to strengthen supplier relationships, manage risk, negotiate strategically, and focus on higher-impact business decisions.

Businesses need faster decisions, but they can't keep adding people to make them. As AI begins handling supply chain transactions that once required human intervention, companies face a question that goes well beyond efficiency.

What happens to the people who once made those decisions?

According to Brooks Noonan, vice president at NTT DATA, the answer could be an unexpected opportunity. Supply chain professionals could spend less time managing transactions such as purchase orders and more time negotiating supplier contracts, strengthening relationships, and making decisions with greater consequences for the business.

On a recent episode of The Future of Supply Chain podcast, Noonan shares his vision of how autonomous execution, real-time intelligence, and human expertise could transform supply chain operations, and the roles people play in them.

AI is moving from monitoring supply chains to making decisions

For years, digital supply chains have focused on visibility. Dashboards help companies monitor inventory, identify disruptions, and track supplier performance. Armed with that information, employees determine what action to take and then carry it out.

Now, AI is beginning to change that division of labor.

"What we're seeing is the shift to truly that autonomous piece, and that starts to focus on execution, versus the visibility," Noonan explains.

AI agents can move beyond identifying supply chain problems to executing certain decisions in real time, within established guardrails. For teams managing constant changes in demand, supply, inventory, and logistics, that shift could make a considerable difference.

Take, for example, a consumer goods manufacturer facing a potential stockout at one of its distribution centers. An AI-powered system could evaluate inventory across the network, incoming production, customer demand, transportation capacity, lead times, and service priorities, then determine the best available response.

Rather than simply alerting a planner to the issue, the AI agent could act within predefined limits, reallocating available inventory or adjusting shipments to maintain service. That could give supply chain teams more time to focus on major disruptions, trade-offs, and longer-term improvements.

Noonan sees an opportunity to "bring your personnel out of that grunt work and move them more to a strategic initiative."

The bigger opportunity belongs to humans

Giving AI more responsibility for everyday operations could also change how much influence people have over the business.

Noonan sees companies beginning to automate what he calls "high frequency, high complexity micro decisions." These decisions involve numerous variables and occur repeatedly, making them promising candidates for autonomous execution.

"Your human interactions are still going to be on those high consequence, big macro decisions," he explains. Choosing strategic suppliers and negotiating long-term contracts requires experience, judgment, and an understanding of relationships that extend beyond individual transactions.

The timing matters. Most manufacturers still rely on weekly or monthly planning cycles, even as tariffs, customer demand, and supply conditions change rapidly.

Suppose market conditions change on Monday, and a competitor responds on Tuesday. A company waiting until its next weekly planning meeting may already have lost valuable ground.

"Nowadays, even that is much too slow," Noonan warns. "Real-time responsiveness is no longer a luxury or a nice-to-have. It's the cost of admission."

For business leaders, this changes how they might think about productivity. With AI responding to frequent operational changes, employees could devote more attention to selecting suppliers, negotiating long-term agreements, and anticipating risks that threaten future growth.

Can employees trust AI to make the right call?

Giving AI the authority to execute inventory decisions means asking employees to trust systems making decisions they once controlled. For business leaders, the challenge is building that confidence while holding the technology accountable for results.

Noonan has seen what happens when confidence is missing. Employees who question the reliability of their company's data often return to familiar offline processes, leaving them disconnected from the latest information.

"It's as much a change management for personnel and people as it is change management of the system," he explains.

For companies introducing autonomous AI, that means investing in training, communication, and hands-on testing. Employees need opportunities to understand how the technology works and develop confidence in the information driving its decisions.

Earning employees' confidence is essential, but executives will ultimately judge these investments by their impact on the business.

Consider a lumber company Noonan has worked with, where expensive raw materials deteriorate over time. Reducing inventory by just two or three days could represent tens of millions of dollars in savings, provided customer service levels remain high.

The example shows why Noonan encourages companies to look beyond hours saved or purchase orders processed. "What I would suggest is, focusing on the balance sheet," he says. Measures such as days inventory outstanding and on-time, in-full delivery can reveal where AI investments have the potential to improve financial and operational performance.

The future of supply chains is making disruptions invisible

The next competitive advantage in supply chain management could come from solving disruptions before customers ever feel their impact.

"The future of supply chain really belongs to those organizations that can replace the static, slow-moving plans with dynamic execution networks," Noonan predicts.

That future depends on supply chains capable of sensing changes and acting on them as they unfold. Companies that can respond immediately could protect customer relationships and maintain service levels even when conditions shift unexpectedly.

As AI takes over more supply chain decisions, people could gain something more valuable: the time and influence to strengthen supplier relationships, anticipate risks, and shape where their businesses go next.
