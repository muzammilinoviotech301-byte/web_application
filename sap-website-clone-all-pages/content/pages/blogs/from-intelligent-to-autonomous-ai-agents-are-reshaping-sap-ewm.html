From Intelligent to Autonomous: AI Agents are reshaping SAP EWM

The warehouse always had the intelligence. What changes now is Agentic AI making it autonomous.

In Part 1 of this series, we looked at the AI already running inside SAP Extended Warehouse Management: Joule for conversational warehouse operations, machine learning for predictive labor demand planning and product slotting, and SAP Signavio for turning process data into prioritized action. That is the intelligence layer. AI that informs and advises.

This post is about what comes next. AI that does not just tell your team what is happening but acts on it while keeping within boundaries your business defines and with every decision fully traceable.

From Insight to Action

There is a practical difference between AI that surfaces information and AI that responds to it. In a warehouse, that distinction plays out in real time. By the time a shift briefing flags a resource gap, the window to rebalance may already be closed. By the time an exception report surfaces a blocked outbound delivery, the customer delivery is already at risk.

The step forward is AI that operates continuously, sensing conditions across warehouse and transportation processes, making structured decisions within the limits the business sets, and acting with a complete audit trail. Human judgment stays central. Teams define the guardrails, step in when judgment is needed, and retain full visibility of every decision taken. What changes is the speed and consistency of the response.

The Logistics Assistant: SAP’s AI Coordination Layer for Logistics

SAP’s Logistics Assistant is the operational layer for this shift. It is not a single capability but an orchestrator with a growing set of specialized Agentic AI capabilities that work across warehousing, fulfillment, and transportation to sense disruptions, make decisions, and execute actions in near real time.

The business case is grounded in operational reality. SAP estimates the Logistics Assistant can deliver up to a 4% reduction in warehouse labor cost, a 3% reduction in transportation cost, and a 6% improvement in on-time delivery performance, representing a significant annual bottom-line benefit.

How SAP EWM is Building the Autonomous Warehouse

SAP is delivering AI agents in EWM across five core warehouse process domains, orchestrated by the Logistics Assistant and progressively extending the reach of autonomous execution, from the first Agentic capabilities available today in phased General Availability to a comprehensive portfolio covering the full operational lifecycle.

Workload and resource management.  When demand is unpredictable and workloads shift mid-shift, manual planning leaves gaps that only become visible after throughput has already suffered. SAP EWM addresses this domain with two AI agents that are available today in phased General Availability.  They forecast workloads by shift, identify resource shortages before they create bottlenecks, and recommend reallocation across activity areas to keep labor aligned with demand.

Receiving and inbound processing.  Errors in delivery documents and missing master data can stall receiving operations before they begin, driving up manual correction effort and extending dock-to-stock time. Agents will validate delivery documents against purchase orders, automate the resulting postings, and give teams the visibility they need to keep inbound operations moving without unnecessary interruption.

Stock management, replenishment, and inventory control.  Static replenishment rules cannot keep pace with dynamic picking demand, and undetected stock discrepancies compound into broader process failures. Agents will monitor stock positions continuously, perform dynamic replenishment of fixed bins and pick locations based on real-time stock levels, manage material reservations through natural language, conduct risk-based inventory planning to reduce manual cycle count effort, and track batch expiration, triggering corrective action before waste or stockout occurs.

Outbound processing and delivery performance.  Manual coordination of picking queues, task assignments, and outbound exceptions creates overhead that grows with volume and puts customer commitments at risk. Agents will monitor outbound flows continuously, automate task bundling and queue assignment, detect problem patterns before they escalate into delays, and resolve issues autonomously, so teams spend less time firefighting and more time on the exceptions that genuinely require human judgment. When an Agent encounters a situation outside its configured authority, it will immediately escalate, surfacing it to the right person with the full context needed to act.

Freight, transportation, and cross-process issue resolution.  Disconnected freight planning, static consolidation rules, and reactive issue management drive up transportation cost and slow dispatch cycles. Agents in the broader portfolio of the Logistics Assistant will recommend and simulate dispatch decisions in real time, support dynamic carrier assignment, guide transportation configuration through conversation, and continuously monitor for blocked processes across logistics operations, resolving issues before they affect service levels.

The SAP EWM roadmap extends this further with AI agents for receiving and dock operations, stock positioning and bin management, automated replenishment, wave and order orchestration, and physical inventory management. Together, these domains form the operational blueprint for the autonomous warehouse built on SAP EWM.

Custom AI Capabilities from SAP Services

Beyond the standard portfolio, SAP Services has developed a set of custom Agentic AI capabilities for EWM customers that address specific operational challenges not yet covered by the standard release. These are built on the SAP Business AI Platform and delivered through SAP Services engagements.

Across inbound operations, three capabilities are currently available. The first uses AI to extract information directly from delivery notes and mail attachments, automatically generating inbound deliveries in SAP S/4HANA and eliminating manual keying. The second gives warehouse operators real-time visibility of handling units at clearing stations and uses Joule to diagnose the root cause of exceptions, improving exception-resolution productivity. The third proactively surfaces incomplete or incorrect product master data ahead of receiving operations, preventing the kind of data issues that stall putaway and slow warehouse throughput.

For production and outbound operations, three further capabilities are available. One correlates warehouse staging tasks with live manufacturing orders, runs resource checks against production deadlines, and automatically recalculates warehouse order priorities when the schedule changes, thereby reducing production line stoppages from late component staging. A second assigns staging lanes and dock doors dynamically based on transport order priority, reducing congestion and truck dwell time. A third provides shift-level visibility of picking and packing workloads and recommends how to allocate resources to prevent bottlenecks before they affect on-time shipping performance.

Human Oversight: Built In, Not Bolted On

A common question from operations leaders is whether AI that acts means a loss of control. Operations leaders consistently favor AI that augments their judgment over AI that replaces it. The goal of autonomous warehouse operations is not to remove people from the loop, but to free them from the routine so they can focus on decisions that genuinely require human expertise. SAP’s design reflects this directly. The level of autonomy is a business decision, not a technology constraint, and it can be different for every process, every scenario, and every team.

Every Agentic AI capability operates within guardrails the business defines. Before acting, AI shows its reasoning: what data it used, what it concluded, and what it is about to do. Confidence scores indicate how certain the system is. Teams decide how much authority each capability holds. For example, a workload rebalancing recommendation might require a supervisor’s confirmation, whereas a routine fixed-bin replenishment in a well-understood scenario might not. Actions taken autonomously can be reversed. There is no one-way door. And every decision, whether made by AI or confirmed by a human, produces a complete audit log that shows exactly what happened and why.

For teams building confidence before granting operational authority, SAP supports a shadow mode wherein AI runs in the background, makes its assessments, but does not act, giving teams a window to observe, verify, and calibrate before going live. Businesses can also run AI capabilities in a sandbox environment first, observing behavior and validating guardrails without any risk to production operations. This governance-by-design approach is what makes AI deployable in warehouse environments where compliance, traceability, and accountability are non-negotiable.

The Autonomous Warehouse is Being Built

The autonomous warehouse is not a future state. SAP EWM is building it today.

For organizations already running SAP EWM, the path forward does not require replacing what you have. The intelligence layer of conversational AI, machine learning, and process intelligence described in Part 1 is the foundation. The Agentic AI capabilities described here extend its reach from insight to action.

The distance between the intelligent warehouse and the autonomous warehouse is shorter than most operations leaders expect.

The future of warehouse management is not coming. It is running.
