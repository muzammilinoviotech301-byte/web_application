Beyond visibility: How AI is driving the shift to autonomous logistics

What happens when logistics stop reacting and starts anticipating? Discover how AI and autonomous systems could transform supply chain decisions, manage disruptions, and reshape the logistics workforce of tomorrow.

Logistics companies have spent decades investing in technology that tells them where shipments are and when something goes wrong. Yet all that visibility hasn't eliminated the costly disruptions their employees must manage.

"Visibility is fundamentally reactive," says Christian Sachse, director at PwC Germany's center of excellence for logistics transformation.

Now, AI could change the equation. As intelligent systems learn to anticipate disruptions, evaluate alternatives, and take action, logistics companies may need fewer people managing routine problems and more employees capable of making complex decisions.

On a recent episode of The Future of Supply Chain podcast, Sachse explores how autonomous logistics could reshape operations, workforce requirements, and the role of human expertise.

Visibility alone won't save your shipments

"At its core, logistics is really about managing the unplanned," Sachse explains. Geopolitical tensions, labor shortages, shifting trade relationships, and everyday transportation problems can disrupt carefully coordinated operations. Even a misplaced pallet or traffic accident can throw delivery schedules off course.

A late vessel, for example, can affect warehouse operations, transportation arrangements, and customer commitments. Logistics teams must decide whether to change routes, arrange another carrier, or adjust delivery plans, often while weighing costs against service expectations.

Those choices become harder when up against deadlines. A delay identified several days in advance may leave time to secure alternative transportation, while the same options may disappear once a vessel misses its arrival window.

Sachse sees an opportunity to move "from anticipation to simulation" as AI helps logistics teams evaluate alternatives before disruptions spread. Professionals can then spend more time considering their options rather than investigating every exception manually.

As AI handles more analysis and routine execution, logistics leaders will need to reconsider how their teams work and which decisions still require human expertise.

AI changes who makes logistics decisions

There are three stages in the transition toward more autonomous logistics. Consider a common scenario where a vessel is traveling from Asia to Europe while other ships along the same route experience delays.

With anticipation, AI analyzes those patterns and predicts whether the vessel is likely to encounter similar problems. Logistics professionals receive advance warnings and have time to prepare before the shipment is delayed.

Simulation takes that first step further. AI evaluates potential responses, including changing shipping routes or switching transportation modes. Planners can review the alternatives, weigh their operational priorities, and decide how to proceed.

The final stage, preemption, is even more ambitious. Sachse envisions autonomous systems analyzing changing conditions, selecting responses, and initiating action without someone managing every routine decision.

Yet, these changes won't be confined to logistics systems. Sachse predicts the supply chain workforce will become "smaller in headcount, significantly higher in capability," too.

For leaders already navigating labor shortages and increasingly complex operations, this prediction presents both a challenge and an opportunity. As AI agents, robotics, and automation take on repetitive tasks, employees may spend more time applying their expertise to work requiring judgment and skill.

PwC's client work shows what targeted logistics improvements can already be delivered. One engagement reduced freight spending by 12%, achieving a return on investment in less than a year. Another cut freight invoice verification process costs by nearly 40% through automation, while a warehouse initiative reduced turnaround time by 30%.

Not every improvement requires advanced AI. Sachse emphasizes identifying the business problem and expected benefits before selecting technology.

Smaller teams will need greater expertise

"Interconnection and collaboration" will play a greater role as logistics networks become more automated, according to Sachse. That means rethinking how people and systems make decisions, share responsibilities, and respond to unexpected challenges.

As AI takes on more routine analysis and execution, logistics professionals could spend more time weighing the costs and consequences of alternative routes, coordinating with carriers and suppliers, and making decisions when service commitments are at risk.

The opportunity extends beyond efficiency. Better-connected operations and increasingly autonomous systems could give companies more flexibility to address disruptions before customer commitments are affected.

The logistics leaders of tomorrow may manage smaller teams with far greater capabilities. Those who succeed will know how to combine autonomous execution with human expertise, responding to disruptions while competitors are still waiting for an alert.
