The Manager 2.0: Reimagining people management in the age of AI

As AI takes on more of the administrative and operational work traditionally performed by managers, organizations should be contemplating a bigger question than just how to augment the current manager role with AI: how should people management be redesigned so it creates mutual value for employees and organizations in the future? Drawing on global research with employees, people managers, and HR leaders coupled with a large-scale job description analysis, this research explores possible futures for managers that challenge common assumptions about managerial work and considers the talent management implications for the new role of people manager in the age of AI.

Fast facts

Signals that the traditional people manager role is shifting

  • 48%

    of employees believe people managers will be replaced by AI in the next five years

  • 50%

    of managers would feel positively about being moved into a role without direct reports

But, what remains “uniquely human” in the age of AI?

  • 45%

    of HR leaders believe manager-provided empathy and emotional support could never be replaced by AI

  • 41%

    of HR leaders believe human connection and relationship building could never be replaced by AI

What do human managers provide that AI could never replace?

Transforming people management

Managers serve the crucial role of the link between the executives and front line, ensuring organizational goals are achieved while recognizing and supporting employees as whole people with unique skills and interests. But the people manager role is rapidly changing due to automation and elevated employee expectations. How can HR technology enable people managers to meet the evolving demands of their role?

Fast facts

Everyone believes that people manager roles should be more exclusive in the future—especially the members of their teams.

  • 69%

    of individual contributors feel that not everyone is capable of being a people manager

  • 46%

    of individual contributors feel that even with the right training, not everyone can learn how to be a good people manager

  • 87%

    of individual contributors feel that companies should be more selective about who they hire/promote to be people managers

  • 80%

    of individual contributors think that some people managers should not be people managers

When a manager moves on—gets promoted or leaves—we take the highest performing individual contributor and say, 'Right. You're the best at that job, so manage that team.' And they were the best at their job, but that does not make them the best manager, or even competent.

Global director of HR systems at a consumer products organization
