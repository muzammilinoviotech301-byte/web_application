What is sustainable procurement?

Sustainable procurement helps you balance cost, risk, and ESG impact across your supply chain.

Published on November 5, 2026

Sustainable procurement definition

Sustainable procurement is the practice of integrating environmental, social, and governance (ESG) criteria into every purchasing decision your team makes. It goes beyond price and quality to consider the full impact of what you buy, who you buy it from, and how those suppliers operate.

When you practice sustainable procurement, you weigh factors like carbon emissions, labor practices, circular metrics, and ethical sourcing alongside traditional cost, quality, and supplier performance metrics.

The goal is to make purchasing decisions that create long-term value for your business, suppliers, and society while conserving resources and protecting communities.

Sustainable procurement is not a separate initiative added to existing procurement processes. It is a core approach to purchasing that builds sustainability into sourcing, supplier management, spend intelligence, and external workforce decisions. By making sustainability part of how you buy, you can manage risk, meet regulatory requirements, and build more resilient supply chains.

Why sustainable procurement matters now

Supply chains face more scrutiny than ever. Investors, customers, regulators, and employees all expect companies to understand and take responsibility for the social and environmental impact of their purchasing decisions. A supply chain is only as responsible as the suppliers within it, and most of a company's environmental and social impact lives in its supply chain, not its direct operations.

Several forces are driving the shift toward sustainable procurement:

  • Regulatory pressure is rising. New laws in the European Union (EU), the United States, and other regions require companies to report on ESG performance, conduct supplier due diligence, and prove their supply chains are free from deforestation and human rights violations. These include the EU Corporate Sustainability Reporting Directive (CSRD), EU Corporate Sustainability Due Diligence Directive (CSDDD), California’s SB 253 and SB 261, Modern Slavery Acts and the Uyghur Forced Labor Prevention Act (UFLPA), the EU Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM), the EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR), and Extended Producer Responsibility (EPR) plastic taxes. Increasingly, businesses face penalties for non-compliance and are being made to bear negative externality costs.
  • Stakeholder expectations are changing. Investors increasingly factor ESG risks into their investment decisions. Fund managers evaluate the ESG performance of the companies they invest in and hold. An example is a company with high incidences of human rights abuses and environmental damage, which could lead to expensive lawsuits and reputational damage, impacting the stock price. Another is a company’s level of compliance with carbon regulations and taxes being paid, where good ESG scores translate to lower carbon costs paid out. Besides regulatory mandates, executive leaders and financial institutions are increasingly seeing useful business cases for supporting environmentally and socially responsible businesses.
  • Supply chain risk is growing.  Global logistics are becoming more vulnerable and disrupted, leading to government policies designed to reduce reliance on fossil fuel supply chains, trade with countries carrying adverse human rights records, imports on critical minerals and commodities, and the impacts of climate disruptions from international trade.
  • Competitive advantage is at stake. Companies that make sustainability part of procurement can reduce costs, build stronger supplier relationships, and differentiate themselves in the market. Naturally, if a company can reduce energy and material waste, it can achieve cost savings.

Sustainable procurement helps organizations reduce supply chain risk, comply with ESG regulations, lower environmental impact, strengthen supplier relationships, and improve long-term business resilience.

Benefits of sustainable procurement:

  • Reduced supply chain risk
  • Improved ESG compliance
  • Lower Scope 3 emissions
  • Stronger supplier relationships
  • Better spend visibility
  • Enhanced brand reputation

The three pillars of sustainable procurement

Sustainable procurement rests on three interconnected pillars: environmental, social, and economic and governance. Together, they form a framework, often called the “triple bottom line,” which essentially looks at business decisions through the lens of environmental, social, and economic outcomes.

Environmental

The environmental pillar focuses on reducing the ecological footprint of your supply chain. This includes measuring and managing Scope 3 procurement emissions, the indirect emissions that come from your suppliers, improving purchases for recycled plastics, and sourcing products responsibly such as licensed wood and timber products. Scope 3 emissions often represent the largest portion of a company's carbon footprint, making them a critical area for action.

Environmentally sustainable procurement practices include:

  • Sourcing from suppliers that use renewable energy or reduce waste.
  • Choosing products and materials with lower carbon footprints.
  • Avoiding goods linked to deforestation, such as those covered by the EU Deforestation Regulation.
  • Prioritizing circular economy principles like recycling, reuse, and reduced packaging.

By addressing environmental impact in your procurement decisions, you can reduce emissions, comply with reporting requirements, and support your company's climate goals.

Social

The social pillar addresses the human impact of your supply chain. It covers labor practices, human rights, health and safety, and supplier diversity. When you practice social sustainable procurement, you look beyond cost to ask whether your suppliers treat their workers fairly and contribute to their communities.

Key social considerations include:

  • Preventing forced labor, child labor, and modern slavery in your supply chain.
  • Promoting supplier diversity by sourcing from minority-owned, women-owned, and small businesses.
  • Supporting safe working conditions throughout your supplier base.
  • Supporting fair wages and ethical labor practices.

Social procurement helps you meet human rights due diligence requirements, protect your reputation, and create positive social impact through your purchasing power.

Economic and governance

The economic and governance pillar focuses on the financial and ethical foundations of your procurement decisions. It covers anti-corruption, fair competition, transparency, and the long-term economic viability of your suppliers.

Sustainable procurement is the process of incorporating economic and governance criteria to purchasing decisions to reduce risk, meet compliance requirements, and create long-term business value.

This pillar includes:

  • Conducting supplier due diligence to assess financial stability and ethical practices.
  • Enforcing anti-bribery and anti-corruption policies across your supply base.
  • Supporting fair competition and ethical sourcing in your bidding processes.
  • Building long-term, mutually beneficial supplier relationships.

Strong governance helps you reduce risk, maintain compliance, and build trust with stakeholders. It also supports the long-term sustainability of your supply chain by helping your suppliers operate responsibly and remain viable partners.

Sustainable procurement regulations and compliance

Regulations are one of the strongest drivers of sustainable procurement. A wave of new laws and frameworks around the world now require companies to understand, report on, and take responsibility for their supply chain impacts.

Key regulations and frameworks include:

  • CSRD: Requires large companies to report on ESG topics, including supply chain impacts and 15 categories of Scope 3 emissions in compliance with European Sustainability Reporting Standards (ESRS).
  • CSDDD: Requires companies to identify, prevent, and mitigate human rights and environmental risks in their operations and supply chains.
  • EUDR: Requires companies to prove that certain commodities do not come from recently deforested land. Learn more with this EU Deforestation Regulation guide.
  • Human rights due diligence legislation such as the German Supply Chain Due Diligence Act (LkSG), Modern Slavery Acts, and the UFLPA: Requires companies exceeding size and/or revenue thresholds to conduct due diligence on suppliers and publish their findings in statement declaration reports.
  • California SB 253 and SB 261: Require large companies doing business in California to disclose greenhouse gas emissions and climate-related financial risks.
  • ISSB IFRS S2: An international framework for sustainability-related financial disclosures, including Scope 3 emissions.

These regulations share a common theme: they push companies to look beyond their direct operations and take responsibility for what happens in their supply chains.

Sustainable procurement helps you meet these requirements by embedding due diligence, reporting, and risk management into your purchasing processes.

Key challenges in sustainable procurement

While the case for sustainable procurement is clear, putting it into practice comes with its challenges:

  • Data quality and availability: Gathering accurate ESG data from suppliers is difficult. Many suppliers lack the systems to measure and report their environmental and social performance, leaving gaps in your sustainability picture.
  • Supplier transparency: Supply chains are often multi-tier and opaque. You may know your direct suppliers, but visibility into their suppliers, and the risks further upstream, is limited.
  • Balancing cost and sustainability: Procurement teams face pressure to control costs while also meeting sustainability goals. Finding the right balance requires clear policies, good data, and tools that help evaluate trade-offs.
  • Regulatory complexity: With regulations varying by region and evolving rapidly, keeping up with compliance requirements is an ongoing challenge.
  • Change management: Shifting from price-driven to sustainable procurement requires new skills, processes, and mindsets across your teams.

Core capabilities of sustainable procurement software

Sustainable procurement applications help make sustainability part of your current workflows. Rather than treating sustainability as a separate initiative, teams can apply sustainability requirements at the moment a purchasing request enters the process, helping guide decisions before sourcing or contracting begins.

Core capabilities of these applications include:

  • Supplier assessment and ESG scoring: Evaluate suppliers against environmental, social, and governance criteria using questionnaires and third-party data from partners like EcoVadis and IntegrityNext.
  • Spend visibility and analysis: Connect spend data with sustainability metrics to understand where your money goes and what impact it has.
  • Risk monitoring: Track supplier risks across multiple dimensions, including financial, environmental, social, and compliance, and get alerts when issues arise.
  • Scope 3 emissions management: Collect and analyze emissions data from suppliers to support reporting and reduction goals.
  • Contract compliance: Incorporate ESG requirements into contracts so suppliers are held accountable for sustainability commitments.
  • AI-supported workflows: Use AI to simplify processes, evaluate supplier responses, support sustainability-driven category strategies, and recommend sustainable options based on policy and available data.

AI plays a supporting role in sustainable procurement applications. It helps teams work more efficiently by surfacing relevant information, evaluating supplier data, and suggesting sustainable alternatives, but people remain at the center of decision-making. AI supports your team; it does not replace their judgment.

Sustainable procurement use cases

Companies across industries are using sustainable procurement to reduce risks and improve compliance.

In fact, when they make sustainability part of procurement, they often discover benefits that extend beyond ESG reporting. When teams have access to the right information at the right time, they can identify potential issues earlier and adapt as supplier or business needs change.

Over time, those decisions add up. Sustainability influences how suppliers are evaluated, how purchases are made, and how businesses work toward their broader goals.

FAQ

  • What are the three pillars of sustainable procurement?

    The three pillars of sustainable procurement are environmental, social, and economic and governance. The environmental pillar focuses on reducing ecological impact, including Scope 3 emissions. The social pillar addresses labor practices, human rights, and supplier diversity. The economic and governance pillar covers ethical sourcing, anti-corruption, and supplier due diligence. Together, they form the triple bottom line, a framework for evaluating the full impact of purchasing decisions.

  • What are the seven principles of sustainable procurement?

    The seven principles of sustainable procurement are: (1) vision and strategy, (2) governance, (3) people, (4) engagement and collaboration, (5) measurement and reporting, (6) supply chain risk and compliance, and (7) innovation. These principles guide organizations in building a structured approach to sustainable procurement, from setting a clear vision to measuring progress and driving continuous improvement.

  • What is the difference between sustainable procurement and sustainable sourcing?

    The difference between sustainable procurement and sustainable sourcing is that sustainable sourcing is a subset of sustainable procurement. Sourcing focuses specifically on the upstream process of identifying, evaluating, and selecting suppliers based on sustainability criteria. Sustainable procurement is broader. It covers the entire purchasing lifecycle, including sourcing, supplier management, contract compliance, spend analysis, and external workforce management. In short, sustainable sourcing is about who you buy from; sustainable procurement is about how you buy across the full process.

  • What regulations require sustainable procurement?

    Key regulations that require sustainable procurement include the CSRD, the CSDDD, the EUDR, modern slavery acts (such as the Australian and United Kingdom Modern Slavery Acts, the UFLPA, and the LkSG), and California SB 253 and SB 261. International frameworks like ISSB IFRS S2 also drive sustainable procurement by requiring companies to report on supply chain emissions and ESG impacts. However, regulations are subject to change.

  • What is ESG in procurement?

    ESG in procurement means applying environmental, social, and governance criteria to purchasing decisions. Environmental criteria cover emissions, resource use, and ecological impact. Social criteria cover labor practices, human rights, and supplier diversity. Governance criteria cover ethics, compliance, and transparency. ESG procurement helps organizations manage risk, meet regulatory requirements, and create long-term value through more responsible purchasing.

  • What is a sustainable procurement policy?

    A sustainable procurement policy is a formal document that defines how your business integrates sustainability into purchasing decisions. It sets out the ESG criteria your buyers should consider, the supplier’s due diligence requirements, and the standards suppliers must meet. A strong policy aligns procurement with your company's sustainability goals, provides clear guidance for buyers, and creates a framework for measuring and reporting progress. An example is making it compulsory for suppliers to agree to the buyer’s code of conduct when supplying goods and services and agreeing to disclose human rights records periodically.
