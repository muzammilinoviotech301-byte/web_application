What are the origins and history of AI?

Artificial intelligence refers to computer systems built to perform tasks that traditionally require human intelligence, such as learning, reasoning, pattern recognition, problem-solving, and decision-making. AI underpins many of today’s most transformative digital experiences, from real-time translation and recommendations to automation, voice assistants, and predictive business analytics.

The vision of intelligent machines has roots in philosophy and mathematics. The term "artificial intelligence" originated in 1956 at a scientific conference held at Dartmouth College. One of AI’s founding fathers, Marvin Minsky, described it as “the science of making machines do things that would require intelligence if done by men.” Modern AI has rapidly accelerated thanks to pioneers like Alan Turing, who introduced the “Turing Test” for machine intelligence, and John McCarthy, who coined the term “artificial intelligence” and established its study as a scientific field in the 1950s. Since then, advances in computing, data, and algorithm design have taken AI from theory to practice, transforming nearly every industry and facet of daily life.

Types and levels of AI

Artificial intelligence comes in several forms, each defined by its capabilities and the ways it supports humans in solving real-world problems. Today’s most powerful business AI solutions—such as those found in SAP applications—are focused on narrowly defined tasks, including predicting demand, recognizing images, or automating repetitive processes. These systems work in tandem with employees, enhancing productivity, reducing errors, and providing the insights necessary for informed decision-making.​

AI by capability

More general or autonomous forms of AI, which could theoretically match or surpass a human’s broad intelligence, remain the subject of academic research and responsible debate. Understanding how AI complements human strengths can help organizations adopt these technologies responsibly and achieve meaningful outcomes. Narrow AI

The most common type encountered in daily life and business is narrow AI, also known as weak AI. These systems tackle specific tasks, such as recognizing speech, analyzing images, and making recommendations. In business, narrow AI powers chatbots, predictive analytics, and intelligent automation, helping to drive efficiency and accuracy in complex processes.​

General AI

General AI represents a theoretical future where machines could seamlessly adapt, learn, and reason across any field, matching the breadth of human intelligence. While ongoing research explores what might be possible, general AI does not exist today. Instead, advances in deep learning and data integration continue to expand the capabilities of specialized AI systems.​

How does artificial intelligence work?

AI uses large datasets to identify patterns, learn from experience, and make informed decisions. In a business context, data is collected and used to train an AI model; the trained model is then deployed for AI inference—meaning it applies what it has learned to new, unseen data to generate predictions or decisions in real-world conditions with speed, precision, and adaptability.

Machine learning

Machine learning models learn from historical data and improve over time, identifying trends and making predictions.

Deep learning

Deep learning uses complex neural networks to recognize patterns in images, speech, or other data, enabling applications such as image recognition and voice assistants.

Neural networks

Neural networks are a specific type of machine learning architecture that excels at processing vast and complex datasets. They power sophisticated solutions for forecasting, customer insights, risk analysis, and personalization.

Natural language processing (NLP)

NLP enables computers to understand and respond to human language, facilitating the development of intelligent chatbots and language translation systems.

Generative AI

Generative AI creates new content, such as text, images, or code, based on prompts, enabling next-generation creativity and productivity.

AI inference

AI inference refers to the process of applying a trained AI model to fresh, real-world data in order to generate predictions or classifications in business workflows. For example, after a neural network is trained on historic sales or transaction data, it can infer likely outcomes for new sales leads or detect anomalies as they occur, driving operational efficiency and better decision-making.

AI applications

Artificial intelligence powers a diverse range of applications, enabling businesses to operate faster, smarter, and more resiliently through automation, prediction, and enhanced experiences.​

Everyday examples

These points demonstrate how AI already appears in everyday tools and services people use at home and at work, often without them realizing it is AI-supported.

  • Digital assistants
    Voice-powered tools like Siri, Alexa, and Google Assistant help with reminders, scheduling, and hands-free device control, streamlining both work and home routines.​
  • Personalized recommendations
    Streaming platforms (Netflix, Spotify) and online retailers use AI to analyze past behavior, offering product and content suggestions tailored to each user.​
  • Image recognition and OCR
    AI systems recognize objects, translate street signs, perform facial recognition for security, and extract text/data from photos or scanned documents.​
  • Autonomous systems
    Self-parking and driver-assist features in cars, warehouse robots, and delivery drones use AI to interpret their surroundings and react in real time.​
  • Chatbots and virtual agents
    Many websites and apps use AI-driven chatbots to answer questions, resolve support issues, and handle routine customer needs 24/7.​
  • Smart home automation
    Thermostats, lighting, and security systems adjust automatically by learning from daily behaviors, contributing to convenience, comfort, and energy savings.​

Core business functions

The following bullets outline how AI supports core business processes, helping teams work faster, reduce errors, and make more informed decisions.

  • Finance: Automate invoice matching, transaction monitoring, fraud detection, risk assessment, and financial forecasting. Machine learning models streamline closing cycles and support audit compliance.​
  • Supply chain and logistics: Power demand forecasting, real-time inventory management, delivery route optimization, quality inspections, and predictive maintenance to help avoid shortages, excesses, and costly downtime.​
  • Procurement: Enhance supplier performance and compliance, automate sourcing, and optimize spend analysis using AI-supported intelligent recommendations and anomaly detection.​
  • Sales and marketing: Personalize customer journeys, drive campaign targeting, and optimize pricing models by analyzing large volumes of customer and market data.​
  • Human resources: Accelerate talent acquisition with AI-driven candidate screening, predict attrition, and support employee engagement and personalized learning.​
  • Customer experience: Deploy conversational AI, chatbots, and recommendation engines to provide fast, personalized assistance and boost satisfaction.​

Industry-specific examples

These examples illustrate how different industries apply AI to solve domain-specific challenges, from equipment reliability to patient care.

  • Manufacturing
    Predict equipment failures before they happen, optimize production lines, enable real-time supply/demand planning, and increase traceability with computer vision and IoT-enabled AI.​
  • Retail
    Facilitate hyper-personalized product offers, automate inventory replenishment, and analyze customer feedback for continuous improvement.​
  • Healthcare
    Support diagnostics, resource scheduling, and personalized treatment recommendations using AI-based analysis of patient data.​
  • Utilities and energy
    Forecast demand, reduce outages, optimize energy distribution, and analyze infrastructure conditions to inform decisions.​

Everyday enterprise applications

The points below focus on common, cross-cutting AI use cases that can be deployed in almost any organization to streamline knowledge work and operations.

  • Document processing
    AI can extract and classify data from invoices, contracts, and reports, reducing manual entry, improving accuracy, and speeding compliance checks.
  • Intelligent search and smart data extraction
    Instantly locate relevant information and files within vast digital archives, empowering faster, more confident decisions across business functions.
  • Automated incident management and IT operations
    AI monitors systems continuously, detects anomalies, and handles incidents automatically to keep critical business applications stable and secure around the clock.
  • Natural language queries
    Users can simply ask business questions in plain language (such as “Show last month’s top-performing products”) and receive immediate insights or visualizations, democratizing access to analytics.
  • Anomaly detection
    AI identifies unusual patterns in transactions, system logs, or user behaviors, supporting fraud prevention, risk management, and early alerting for operations teams.
  • Workflow automation
    From routing customer requests to scheduling maintenance, AI-enhanced automation ensures the right processes run smoothly with minimal manual oversight.

These applications drive smarter, faster, and more reliable outcomes while freeing people to focus on higher-value, creative, and strategic work.

Benefits of AI

Artificial intelligence delivers significant value across industries by transforming productivity, decision-making, customer experiences, and operational outcomes:​

  • Automation and productivity
    AI automates routine tasks such as data entry, invoice processing, and report generation, freeing employees to focus on higher-value strategic work and increasing business productivity.
  • Improved decision-making
    AI-enhanced analytics and predictive models enable faster, more accurate decisions for demand forecasting, financial planning, and risk management, allowing organizations to anticipate market changes and respond proactively.
  • Enhanced customer experience
    Intelligent chatbots, recommendation engines, and personalized interfaces strengthen engagement, speed service response, and help brands build stronger relationships with customers.
  • Cost savings and efficiency
    By optimizing operations such as supply chain management, HR, and procurement, AI can help reduce operational costs, minimize waste, and drive more efficient resource allocation.
  • Innovation and agility
    AI enables organizations to experiment rapidly, support new product launches, and adapt processes swiftly in response to changing market and customer needs.
  • Collaboration and knowledge sharingAI-enhanced tools facilitate cross-team collaboration, making critical information and insights accessible to all key stakeholders.

AI ethics and challenges

As artificial intelligence becomes increasingly embedded in businesses and daily life, it brings both opportunities and responsibilities. Addressing the ethical considerations of AI is essential to ensure technologies remain trustworthy, fair, and secure. Responsible AI design answers key questions such as “Is AI safe?” and “What are the main ethical concerns businesses and society must consider as AI evolves?”

The adoption of AI presents several complex ethical considerations and practical challenges for businesses and society:​

  • Bias and fairness
    AI models can perpetuate and amplify existing biases in training data, potentially leading to unfair outcomes in hiring, lending, or resource allocation. Addressing bias requires ongoing testing, diverse data sources, and transparent development practices.
  • Transparency and explainability
    Many AI algorithms, especially deep learning models, operate as black boxes, making it difficult for users to understand how decisions are made. Building systems that offer clear explanations helps establish trust and regulatory compliance.
  • Data privacy and security
    AI systems often rely on vast amounts of data, raising concerns about privacy, consent, and information security. Companies must establish robust data governance frameworks and comply with regional data regulations.
  • Safety and deepfakes
    Powerful generative AI can create hyper-realistic but fake images, audio, or video (deepfakes), fueling misinformation and risks to privacy, democracy, and brand reputation.
  • Regulation and compliance
    Laws and standards governing AI continue to evolve globally. Staying ahead of regulations helps protect businesses from legal exposure and reinforces ethical use.

Organizations must foster a culture of responsible AI, implementing fair, transparent, and accountable practices while proactively monitoring risks and continuously adapting to the advancement of technologies and evolving societal expectations.
