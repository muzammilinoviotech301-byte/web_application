Data quality and governance in the age of agentic AI: What CIOs and Chief Data Officers need to know right now

There's a version of this conversation that's already happening at the board level in most enterprises. Your teams are deploying AI agents. Some are showing real promise. And somewhere, quietly, someone is asking: "But what happens when the agent gets it wrong?"

The short answer is: it depends entirely on the quality of the data it ran on.

We've spent years talking about data quality and governance as important initiatives. Something for the data team or IT to manage. Important, yes, but rarely urgent enough to compete for C-suite airtime. Agentic AI changes that priority call completely, and fast.

The shift that changes everything

Traditional AI was, at its core, advisory. It surfaced patterns, flagged anomalies, suggested next steps. A human sat in the middle, reviewing outputs and deciding what to do. That human layer was your safety net.

Agentic AI removes the net.

These systems retrieve data, call external tools, trigger workflows, update records, send communications, and route decisions, often without a human ever reviewing the output. That is not a future state. That is what enterprise teams are deploying today.

As TDWI frames it: "The practical question is no longer 'Is the model good enough?' It is 'Is the data environment reliable enough for an AI system to act on it?'" (TDWI, Data Quality Is the Control Plane for Enterprise Agentic AI).

That question cuts straight to the CIO and Chief Data and Analytics Officer's mandate.

What your data teams are already telling you

The persona research that SAP conducted in early 2026 across US and UK data and analytics professionals makes something very clear: the data foundation problems that CIOs and CDOs have been managing for years are already creating friction at the operational level.

Here's what practitioners said, in their own words:

"There isn't a single source of truth in our data for quite a few things. If you speak to analysts within certain departments, and you come and speak to myself, and you ask us both the exact same questions, you will get two separate answers." Business Analyst, Technology & Electronics

"We don't really have a governance structure in our organization, so at the moment we're going through a process of actually assigning data owners and trying to put in some of these fundamental policies and processes." Data Steward, Government

"The speed of change in AI is adding pressure. There's constant expectation to incorporate GenAI, automation, new tools that they see in these conferences, or they read somewhere on LinkedIn, while we are still maintaining that comp." Data Scientist, Healthcare & Pharmaceuticals

These are not edge cases. They describe common conditions across industries. And they describe the exact environment where AI agents are being deployed.

Why agentic AI amplifies, not fixes, data problems

Agents inherit data quality issues and then execute them. At scale. Without judgment.

Your data was designed, implicitly at least, to be interpreted by humans who could recognize when something looked off. A seasoned analyst would question a pricing record that seemed stale. An account manager would notice a dunning notice that shouldn't have gone out. Agents will not. They act on what they find, accurately and consistently, which is exactly the problem when the data is wrong.

Gartner has put numbers to the risk: 52% of enterprises cite data quality as their primary blocker to agent deployment, and 40% of AI agents are projected to fail by 2027 due to governance gaps (The Daily Brief, July 2026, Gartner: 40% of AI Agents Will Fail by 2027—Governance Gap).Those are not theoretical projections. They are already showing up in stalled programs and failed pilots.

Three risk patterns show up most often:

Classical data quality problems, now operational. This includes incomplete records, stale data, and duplicates. Analytics teams have tolerated these for years because a human was always reviewing the output. Remove that review layer and the same problems produce cascading mistakes across connected systems before anyone notices.

Context fragmentation. This is the most underappreciated risk. Research finds that 77% of executives report that only 20% or less of their enterprise data is sufficiently described and contextualized for agents to use (PR Newswire, July 2026, New Research: Why Enterprise Agentic AI Stalls Before It Scales). Agents can read raw field values. They cannot infer business meaning from unlabeled columns, ambiguous status codes, or undocumented relationships. Technically correct data that an agent misinterprets is not better than wrong data. It is wrong data with a clean audit trail.

Ungoverned access. Agents read whatever they can access, including stale, mislabeled, or confidential content in ungoverned repositories. 80% of organizations report their AI agents have already performed actions beyond intended scope. That includes 39% accessing unauthorized systems and 31% inappropriately sharing sensitive data (Non-Human Identify Management Group, December 2025, AI agent governance is colliding with enterprise security controls).

Governance is now your AI control plane

In the agentic era, data governance stops being a compliance function or an IT practice. It becomes the operational control layer that determines how AI behaves, what it can touch, what actions it can take, and what record it leaves behind.

Think about it this way: data quality determines how well an agent performs its task. Data governance determines what the agent is permitted to see, do, and change, and ensures those decisions are traceable and accountable. Both matter. Neither is sufficient without the other.

Governance for agentic AI has to answer four concrete questions that traditional governance often sidesteps:

  1. Who owns each data domain and is accountable for its quality before agents act on it?
  2. What quality thresholds must be met before autonomous action is permitted?
  3. What can each agent access, enforced at runtime, not just documented in policy?
  4. What did the agent do and why, captured in a durable, auditable record?

The regulatory dimension is no longer optional. The EU AI Act's Article 10 explicitly requires governance of data quality, provenance, and sensitivity as a condition of deployment. GDPR supervisory bodies across Europe have published dedicated guidance on agentic AI data protection risks. If your organization operates in financial services, healthcare, or the public sector, your agents are already under scrutiny.

Where do you stand? A data and AI maturity self-assessment

The research finding that may matter most for CIOs and CDOs is this one: engagement strategies should align with an organization's data maturity. Whether an organization is still grappling with legacy systems or incorporating AI-driven capabilities, the conversation and the priorities need to match where the organization actually is.

Here is a practical framework for assessing your current position and identifying the next right move.

Stage 1: Reactive foundation

What it looks like: Data lives across disconnected systems. There is no consistent single source of truth. Different teams produce different answers to the same question. Data ownership is informal or absent. Manual reconciliation is the norm. AI is not yet a serious consideration beyond point solutions.

The tell: Your data stewards (or lack thereof) spend most of their time firefighting, not building. As one data steward put it: "We don't really have a governance structure. We're going through a process of actually assigning data owners."

What needs to happen before agents: Establish data ownership. Assign accountability by domain. Identify and remediate your most critical quality gaps. Agentic AI in this environment will amplify every problem you have.

Stage 2: Consolidation in progress

What it looks like: You are modernizing. Cloud migrations are underway. There is movement toward consolidated platforms and clearer data flows. But legacy systems still coexist with modern infrastructure, and governance policies are early or inconsistent. AI is being used for productivity tasks: code assistance, documentation, anomaly detection.

The tell: Teams are simplifying, but cautiously. The concern is monolithic platform lock-in vs. loss of best-of-breed capability. One enterprise architect described success as: "The right data for the right people at the right time, in the right format." That goal is visible but not yet achieved.

What needs to happen before agents: Prioritize semantic context. Annotate your most business-critical data domains with metadata. Start building the audit capability that agentic actions will require. Define quality thresholds by use case.

Stage 3: Governed intelligence

What it looks like: A unified data platform is in place or close. Data owners are assigned. Lineage is tracked. Governance policies exist and are enforced, at least for core domains. AI is driving real business value in analytics, reporting, and decision support.

The tell: Your data professionals describe success in business terms. "I ensure our systems are as efficient as possible and avoid errors of reporting because the business relies on our data to make strategic decisions" (Business Analyst, Manufacturing). Data is trusted. The question shifts from quality to capability.

What needs to happen before agents: Extend lineage to decision-level traceability. Enforce least privilege access at the agent level, in real time. Establish quality thresholds that gate autonomous action. Pilot agents in bounded, lower-risk domains first.

Stage 4: Agentic AI ready

What it looks like: Data governance is enforced at runtime, not just documented in policy. Business metadata and semantic context are present across core domains. Audit trails capture decision chains, not just data flows. Agents operate within defined guardrails with human oversight on exceptions.

The tell: Your teams are asking the right question, the one TDWI identified: not whether the model is good enough, but whether the data environment is reliable enough for an agent to act on it. And you have a defensible answer.

What to protect: Maintain explainability and reversibility as guardrails. The research is consistent that AI adoption is pragmatic and productivity driven. Practitioners want "explainability, reversibility, and tight controls" as non-negotiables for advanced AI. Your governance framework needs to reflect that.

The bottom line

The organizations extracting durable value from agentic AI are treating data quality and governance as prerequisites to deployment, not afterthoughts. The ones stalling, or quietly managing failures they have not yet disclosed, are the ones that deployed agents before the data foundation was ready.

The pattern is consistent across current industry research: enterprises are deploying agents faster than they are governing the data those agents run on. That is the single most common cause of failed or stalled agentic AI programs, not model capability, but data foundation.

SAP's position is direct: AI agent sprawl, ungoverned agents operating across fragmented, low-quality data estates, is now a board-level risk.

As a CIO or Chief Data and Analytics Officer, you are uniquely positioned to change that. Not by slowing down AI adoption, but by ensuring that the data foundation your agents run on is ready for the work you are asking them to do.

Your AI is only as trustworthy as your data. That has always been true. Agentic AI just makes it impossible to ignore.
