What is a learning management system (LMS)?

An LMS is a software application used for planning, delivering, and tracking training and educational programs. Organizations use LMS software to make learning more engaging and accessible to employees, helping improve employee skills, compliance, retention, and productivity.

LMS meaning and definition

A learning management system, often referred to as an LMS, is a software application that provides organizations with a framework for creating, managing, delivering, and tracking educational courses, training programs, or learning and development programs throughout the employee lifecycle. An LMS uses artificial intelligence (AI) and smart technologies to assess individual and organizational training needs, create personalized learning paths, and track learning and compliance training.

Why is learning management important today?

Major demographic changes, emerging technologies, new regulations, and a highly competitive job market are creating talent gaps and skills shortages. Technology, particularly AI, is leading to new types of jobs, such as prompt engineers, AI trainers, and AI ethicists, and a lack of people trained to perform these jobs. Learning management is essential for driving continuous learning and development—from training through upskilling and reskilling—to help fill these gaps and empower people to drive the business forward.

What’s the difference between upskilling and reskilling?

  • Upskilling helps people develop new or more advanced technology and human skills, including decision-making and problem-solving skills, to perform better in their current roles.
  • Reskilling helps people learn new skills for a completely different role or career change.

As a result, businesses are putting employee skills at the center of HR practices, according to a 2024 HR trends report. Based on a study of 100 reputable business press resources, the report determined that “Reskilling and upskilling initiatives, especially for urgent skills needs like AI, will lead to increased motivation in employees, so it must also see greater prioritization by organizations.”

What are learning management systems used for?

Organizations—from educational institutions to corporations and government agencies—use learning management systems to meet their educational, training, and compliance goals. For example, a university uses an LMS to deliver online courses to its students worldwide, while a government agency uses an LMS to provide the latest compliance training to employees and send reminders until it’s completed. Most organizations use an LMS for:

Onboarding

It’s essential for HR teams to provide consistent and regionally accurate onboarding training from day one. LMS solutions can help HR personalize, automate, and deliver onboarding tasks and development goals quickly and cost-effectively to help new hires hit the ground running.

Employee training

An LMS can help team leaders and learning and development (L&D) professionals get a centralized view of each employee’s assessed skill levels and current and past training activities to help them deliver the right training to the right people—and keep track of their progress and compliance status.

Upskilling and reskilling

Every employee has unique learning styles and needs based on current and future roles and responsibilities. An LMS supports skills-driven learning by using AI and smart technologies to generate personalized learning recommendations and learning paths that align with specific skills, learning styles, and strategic business goals.

Continuing education and professional development

An LMS connects corporate priorities and skills needs with an existing workforce and its training and professional development activities. It can also help employees identify potential career paths and personalized development goals.

Extended enterprise and channel training

From franchisees to gig workers, anyone who represents a brand must be well-versed in operational rules and guidelines. Smart LMS technologies can help companies create training and brand initiation programs that align with the roles and tasks of each vendor, consultant, or external partner.

Compliance training

To help ensure safety and well-being and minimize risk and loss, businesses must take an organized and consistent approach to regulatory and safety compliance. In addition to assigning training based on nearly any criteria and keeping track of training compliance targets, organizations can use an LMS to develop mandatory training packages that employees won’t dread—and may even enjoy!

What are the benefits of using an LMS?

By centralizing and streamlining learning content and delivery, an LMS can help organizations achieve many long- and short-term benefits, such as:

  • Higher course completion rates: Accessible, always-on learning opportunities make it easier for employees to attend training and development programs, complete courses, and maintain compliance on any device.
  • Increased engagement: Interactive and innovative learning options, such as virtual reality trainings and gamification integrated into an LMS, help make learning more appealing and engaging for learners.
  • Time and cost savings: Virtual and on-demand training allows organizations to eliminate travel and off-site expenses (for both employees and instructors) and reduce administrative and training consultancy costs.
  • Continuous learning culture: An LMS can track, recommend, and deliver each new stage or learning unit in a continuous learning journey, encouraging employees to never stop learning.
  • Improved compliance: With capabilities such as workflow monitoring, notification, and e-signature processes, an LMS offers a transparent global view of an organization’s compliance status for training and certification to all stakeholders involved.
  • Future-ready workforce: An LMS uses data and individualized insights to create courses and suggests skills that are relevant and digestible, increasing attendance, completion, and retention. This translates to faster and more strategic upskilling and reskilling of the talent pool.

Challenges of implementing and maintaining an LMS

While there are many benefits of an LMS, organizations should be prepared for the challenges of implementing, managing, and maintaining a learning management system, including:

  • User adoption: Users who are uncomfortable with change or new technology may be slow to adopt and effectively use an LMS.
  • Implementation and maintenance costs: Because the costs of developing, implementing, and maintaining an LMS can be substantial, an LMS may not be affordable for some organizations.
  • Technical issues: Poor connections, compatibility issues, and software bugs can disrupt the learning experience and deter users from finishing courses or starting new ones.
  • Content creation and customization: Additional resources and skills are needed to effectively develop fresh, high-quality content that meets users’ needs and keeps them engaged.
  • Updates and maintenance: To keep an LMS running smoothly and securely, organizations must perform regular maintenance and updates, which could put a burden on resources and create talent gaps that need to be filled.

Encouraging user adoption of an LMS

As with any digital transformation, implementation starts with people first—and a clear and open communication strategy. The initial reaction to news of an LMS implementation may include some pessimism due to longstanding perceptions of traditional training as boring and inconvenient. To get ahead of this, organizations can start their LMS journey by:

  • Customizing the LMS rollout plan to resonate with different teams
  • Building multimedia and access-anywhere components into LMS user training
  • Offering microlearning units, fun and gamified elements, and social and personal exchanges—instead of exhausting, day-long training sessions
  • Demonstrating how an LMS can help users achieve their personal and professional goals, advance their careers, and maintain their compliance

How do learning management systems work?

An LMS works by providing a digital platform for creating, managing, and delivering courses, training programs, or learning and development programs enhanced by AI and smart technologies. Users can access the platform to track training, compliance, or development goals; develop new training courses; complete live and on-demand training from any mobile device; and more.

Common features of an LMS include:

  • AI-enabled
    Organizations can use AI features to generate personalized recommendations for learning, mentors, assignments, career paths, dynamic teams, and more to help close talent gaps, reduce skills shortages, and improve employee satisfaction—quickly and effectively.
  • Intuitive interface
    A user-friendly, customizable interface that’s easy to access and navigate helps speed user adoption and participation.
  • Mobile-friendly
    Today, organizations are not only allowing employees to use personal devices—they’re depending on them to do so. To maximize employee engagement and participation, an LMS enables learners to access and complete learning materials and assessments from any mobile device.
  • Data tracking
    A centralized portal synchronizes learning progress when learners are online, helping HR and L&D professionals track learners’ journeys and progress across departments and regions. By tracking and comparing different learning models, L&D teams can hone and customize training programs.
  • Personalized user experience
    Intelligent LMS solutions supported by AI and machine learning can analyze multiple data sets—including a learner’s history, preferences, and performance—to align with the latest corporate priorities as well as personal skills, needs, and interests to help create personalized learning paths.
  • Centralized learning materials
    Users should be able to easily find and access the full range of learning content for their learning path. An LMS can provide centralized access to learning materials—from on-demand courses to quizzes to documentation—using AI and enhanced search capabilities with filtering options.
  • Flexible reporting and analytics
    An LMS can help tailor reporting and analytics to align with specific criteria and learning objectives. The ability to customize reports in real time and get clear visual representations helps L&D teams spot patterns and get a better idea of specific challenges and ways to improve learning courses and materials.
  • Compliance
    Emerging technologies are driving new laws and legal rules, such as the EU AI Act, reflecting globally changing levels of scrutiny, regulations, and workforce expectations. An LMS can help organizations centralize, organize, and keep track of each employee’s status for mandatory compliance training and certification.
  • Flexible range of assessment tools
    Different metrics require different assessment tools, such as exam engines, branching scenarios, and other types of simulations. With a more robust range of assessment tools, learners can be given feedback that’s actionable and specific to their unique challenges and needs.
  • Course creation
    Flexible course creation options allow users to create a course from scratch by developing a lesson right inside the LMS or by importing existing training material in a wide variety of media—from documents to webinars, videos, and interactive training modules.
  • Learning integrated into the employee lifecycle
    An LMS can help organizations align employee growth goals with organizational skills needs—from onboarding to succession—by setting up development goals and fostering internal mobility through always-on learning opportunities, such as projects, fellowships, coaching, and mentoring.
  • Interoperability
    As learning management systems become more sophisticated, so do the technical standards and data models that support interoperability between learning activities across technical systems. An LMS must be able to integrate with third-party systems and enable the exchange and sharing of information between different systems through e-learning content standards, such as SCORM, xAPI, and Tin-Can.
  • Social learning
    According to a Forbes article on social media statistics and trends in 2024, the average person spends about 2 hours and 25 minutes on social media every day. By engaging learners through social channels, an LMS can help build enthusiasm and increase participation and collaboration.
  • Flexible implementation
    An LMS that offers flexible implementation and configuration can scale to meet an organization’s future business needs and integrate solution add-ons to meet new requirements, such as gamification from third-party partners. This flexibility helps reduce upfront costs and enable ongoing innovation.
  • Ease of integration and use
    By enabling seamless cloud integration with other business systems, an LMS can provide a more accurate and actionable picture of learning and development needs and opportunities—and ease of access for all users.
  • Gamified learning
    In an LMS, gamification keeps learners engaged through points, ratings, badges, and more. When a learner can work toward a specific level or standing, experience shows that they’re more likely to engage with the content and better retain what they’ve learned.
  • Microlearning
    Microlearning is an educational strategy that takes broad or complex topics and breaks them down into bite-sized study units. In an LMS, these units can be viewed whenever and wherever the learner needs a refresher and can be incorporated into more formal or longer-term educational goals.
  • Learning in the flow of work
    By learning in the flow of work, learners can quickly gain access to answers or bite-sized microlearning chunks while they’re working and engaging in a task that’s relevant to the learning unit in question.
  • Open platform
    An LMS solution with an open ecosystem can help organizations take advantage of diverse content from Massive Open Online Course (MOOC) providers, for example, as well as benefit from new innovations from a variety of sources.

Types of learning management systems

Learning management systems are always evolving to meet different business and technology requirements and learner types. By understanding the unique features of each LMS, an organization can select the one that best fits its needs. Types of LMS include:

Cloud LMS (SaaS-based solutions): SaaS-based solutions offer a range of capabilities and integrations with smooth scalability and updates in the cloud. Typically hosted by a software provider, this type of LMS can easily scale up or down based on demand and is more cost-effective to maintain and upgrade than on-premise systems.

Self-hosted LMS: For businesses that wish to install and host their own LMS and content, this system allows for full control but compromises on scalability, speed, costs, and ease of integration.

Open LMS: Open source is great for smaller businesses that are prepared to take the prebuilt free public code and implement it in their own systems.

Mobile LMS: This isn’t a distinct “type” of LMS but rather an LMS that comes with device-friendly components and dashboards (which open and self-hosted systems may not).

Corporate LMS: While an educational LMS can be adapted for corporate use, these systems are designed with corporate users in mind. A corporate LMS can either be a cloud-based or self-hosted system.

Educational LMS: These systems formed the foundation for many of today’s corporate LMS systems but are not natively structured for corporate needs and priorities. An educational LMS, such as Canvas, Blackboard, and Google Classroom, can either be a cloud-based or self-hosted system.

FAQ

  • What does LMS mean?

    LMS stands for learning management system (LMS). An LMS is a software application that helps organizations administer, document, track, report, automate, and deliver educational courses, training programs, or learning and development programs.

  • What is e-learning?

    E-learning is learning that is done outside of a classroom, usually over the Web and often conducted through a cloud-based LMS.

  • What is microlearning?

    Microlearning is a highly focused educational session delivered in small pieces, such as a tutorial about a specific problem. It plays a key role in the gamification of learning.
